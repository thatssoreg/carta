# Jurançon

**Pyrénées-Atlantiques, France**

Jurançon is a white-wine appellation in the Pyrenean foothills south and west of Pau. It is one of the essential homes of Petit Manseng and Gros Manseng and one of the best places to understand the region's defining structural equation: long ripening, high sugar potential, and persistent acidity.

## Landscape and growing conditions

INAO describes hillside viticulture at the foot of the Pyrenees under an oceanic climate. Autumn can be shaped by a warm, dry southerly wind, helping grapes ripen and concentrate after a humid growing season.

Steep slopes and high-trained vines help explain the continued importance of hand harvesting. The appellation is not one uniform soil or exposure, and CARTA's GIS work should eventually make that internal variation visible rather than drawing one flat polygon and calling the job finished.

## Grapes

Petit Manseng and Gros Manseng are the principal local grapes in current INAO material.

- [Petit Manseng](../../../grapes/petit-manseng.md) brings exceptional sugar concentration with persistent acidity.
- [Gros Manseng](../../../grapes/gros-manseng.md) is vigorous, fertile, late-ripening, and central to dry as well as sweet wine.
- [Petit Courbu](../../../grapes/petit-courbu.md) and other local whites belong to the broader regional grape culture even when their exact legal roles differ by current specification.

## Styles

### Sweet

Sweet Jurançon is the historic international reference. Late harvest and concentration can produce substantial sweetness without sacrificing the acid spine that keeps the wines vivid.

### Dry

Dry Jurançon is not a recent invention. INAO dates recognition of the dry category to 1975. Today it is critical to understanding the appellation because Gros Manseng and Petit Manseng can produce powerful, dry, high-acid whites as well as sweet wines.

### Contemporary variation

Some producers use barrel, long lees aging, or other low-intervention approaches. These choices widen the style spectrum, but CARTA does not treat “natural Jurançon” as a separate legal category.

## History & significance

INAO records recognition of sweet Jurançon in 1936 and dry Jurançon in 1975.

The deeper significance is continuity under changing market expectations. Jurançon built prestige through concentrated sweet whites, then increasingly became legible to modern drinkers through dry wines. The same local grape material now supports both histories.

That is why Jurançon matters beyond appellation trivia. It is one of the clearest places to study how grape physiology, climate, tradition, and consumer demand can produce multiple stable style families.

## Producers to know

Representative anchors, not a ranking:

- **Domaine Cauhapé** — long-standing benchmark and useful stylistic reference
- **Clos Uroulat** — important for concentrated Manseng-based whites
- **Domaine de Souch** — strong regional reference
- **Camin Larredya** — especially useful for its explicit work with Gros Manseng, Petit Manseng, Petit Courbu, and Camaralet

## Relationship to nearby appellations

[Pacherenc du Vic-Bilh](pacherenc-du-vic-bilh.md) shares grapes and a broader southwest-French context but is a **different appellation and geography**. Run 02 suggested a wine might qualify as Jurançon or Pacherenc based largely on sugar level. CARTA rejects that simplification.

## Current developments

Dry wine, organic/biodynamic farming, and renewed attention to local grapes are widening how Jurançon is understood outside the region. Petit Manseng's growth in Virginia makes the appellation newly useful as a comparative reference, although CARTA does not yet encode Virginia and Jurançon as proven climate analogues.
