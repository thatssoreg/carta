# Beaujolais

**One appellation, governed mentions, ten crus, many distinct places**

Beaujolais lies immediately south of the Mâconnais and north of Lyon. Gamay overwhelmingly dominates the vineyard, but that shared grape should sharpen attention to legal origin, physical place and producer practice rather than erase their differences.

## How to read the map

The CARTA Beaujolais label is derived from mapped child-appellation geometry and is only an orientation anchor. The broad Beaujolais area, the governed Villages geographical complement and all ten cru areas come from the pinned INAO dataset. ## Current legal structure

The specification homologated in August 2026 treats **Beaujolais** as the appellation name. **Villages** is a mention that may follow Beaujolais; specified commune names may also supplement it. CARTA therefore models Beaujolais Villages as a governed classification/mention with its own INAO source geometry, not as a second standalone appellation.

The ten separate cru appellations are Brouilly, Côte de Brouilly, Chénas, Chiroubles, Fleurie, Juliénas, [Morgon](../appellations/morgon.md), Moulin-à-Vent, Régnié and Saint-Amour.

The current Beaujolais specification enumerates 88 communes in its production area and 38 communes for the Villages mention. The official **2024** regional key figures—11,771 claimed hectares, 12 AOCs in the institutional presentation, 85 wine-growing communes, 96/4 grape share and 94/4/2 color share—remain valid as dated statistics. They are not silently rewritten into 2026 legal counts.

## Grapes and colors

The current Beaujolais specification covers white, rosé and red wines. White is Chardonnay; red and rosé use Gamay N as the principal variety with tightly bounded accessory varieties. The primeur or nouveau mention is available to red and rosé, not white.

Official 2024 regional key figures report a 96% Gamay and 4% Chardonnay grape split, alongside a 94% red, 4% white and 2% rosé production split. These are dated, scoped measures—not timeless definitions or evidence that every cru shares the broad Beaujolais rules.

## A more complex ground story

Granite matters, especially in celebrated northern sites, but “Beaujolais equals granite” is too flat. The official regional terroir program used extensive borings and soil pits to identify hundreds of variants across complex geology. The responsible lesson is to investigate the named place, not predict taste from a regional slogan.

[Mont Brouilly](../../../landscapes/README.md) is recorded as a physical hill related to both Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly; those legal areas remain separate from the hill and from vineyard parcels. Py hill, Morgon and Côte du Py are likewise three different identities. No guessed hill or lieu-dit polygon was added.

## A human route in

[Morgon](../appellations/morgon.md) and [Domaine Marcel Lapierre](../../../producers/domaine-marcel-lapierre.md) provide one strong route into cru identity and the region’s low-intervention history. Jean Foillard, Château Thivin, Domaine de la Grand’Cour and Domaine des Terres Dorées add distinct northern, hill, site, cellar and southern-succession contexts. The [Gang of Four](../../../ecosystems/gang-of-four-beaujolais.md) remains an informal ecosystem, not an appellation or a substitute for individual biographies.

Carbonic maceration, semi-carbonic maceration and whole-cluster fermentation are separate techniques. The 2026 specification describes a tradition inspired by semi-carbonic maceration but does not impose one universal cellar protocol.
